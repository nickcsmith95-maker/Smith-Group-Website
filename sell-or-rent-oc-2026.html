Should You Sell or Rent Out Your Orange County Home in 2026?

By Nick Smith · 2026-09
← Back to all posts

With one in five Orange County listings sitting past 90 days, some owners are asking a different question: What if I just don't sell? Here is what the math actually looks like at today's prices.

Is it better to sell your Orange County home or rent it out in 2026?

Single-family medians are sitting near $1.4M, and roughly one-fifth of active Orange County listings haven't gotten an offer as of late August 2026. That's led some equity-rich homeowners to run the numbers on holding onto their property instead of listing into a market where buyers can afford to be picky. There's no universal answer here. It comes down to your mortgage, your tax situation, and what the property would actually cash flow. But the framework below gets you asking the right questions.

It's a fair question to ask. The August 24, 2026 OC housing report shows active inventory at a 2026 high, with about 20% of listings going stale. Buyers are negotiating harder on price and terms because they can. If you're not in a rush to sell, holding starts to look tempting.

But holding isn't free, and before you turn your primary home into a rental, it's worth stress-testing the idea honestly.

What kind of cash flow could you actually expect renting a $1.4 million home in Orange County?

This is the part most sellers skip past too quickly. Here's roughly how to think it through:

  • Purchase price / current value: around $1.4M for a single-family home, or $798K for a condo or townhome, based on August 2026 OC housing data
  • Gross rent: what your actual property, in your actual neighborhood, would command. Pull a real rental comp for this, not a Zillow estimate
  • Carrying costs: mortgage (if you have one), property taxes, insurance, HOA dues if applicable, a maintenance reserve, and property management if you're not planning to self-manage
  • Subtract the second group from the first, and that's your real cash flow. At Orange County's current cap rates, which Q2 2026 multifamily data puts near 4.72%, that margin tends to be thin

Run a $1.4M property at a 4.72% cap rate and the gross operating income comes in well under what a lot of owners expect. Add a mortgage at today's mid-6% rates, and negative cash flow isn't some worst-case scenario. It's a real possibility.

That doesn't automatically make renting the wrong call. It might just mean you're betting on appreciation rather than income, which is a fine strategy, but a different one, and it calls for a different plan.

Selling gets you liquidity, plus a tax decision worth paying attention to. California's primary residence exclusion, $250K for single filers and $500K for married couples, is real money at these prices. Once you convert the home to a rental, that clock starts running out, and eventually the exclusion goes away entirely.

What actually matters here is your specific numbers: what you'd walk away with after agent fees, prorated taxes, and any repairs, compared to what year one actually looks like as a landlord. Every property is different. The only way to know for sure is to run both scenarios side by side.

Common questions I get about this

Should I sell or rent out my Orange County home in 2026?

Honestly, it depends on your loan balance, your tax picture, and what the place would realistically rent for. Inventory sitting stale makes holding tempting, but a mid-6% mortgage rate against a 4.72% cap rate can eat your margin fast. Run both scenarios with real numbers before deciding either way.

Won't I lose money renting if my mortgage rate is higher than the cap rate?

Possibly, at least on paper. A mortgage rate above the market cap rate is a classic sign that a property will run negative or close to it as a rental, at least in year one. That's not automatically a dealbreaker if you're playing a longer game on appreciation, but you should know the number going in rather than finding out from your bank statement three months later.

What am I giving up tax-wise if I convert my home to a rental instead of selling?

The big one is the primary residence capital gains exclusion, up to $500K tax-free for a married couple filing jointly. That exclusion depends on having lived in the home as your primary residence for a set period, and it doesn't last forever once you move out and start renting. If there's substantial equity built up, that tax math alone can outweigh years of rental income.

What costs am I forgetting as a first-time landlord?

Beyond the mortgage: property taxes, a landlord insurance policy (which is different from your homeowner's policy), HOA dues if you have them, a maintenance reserve for the stuff that always comes up, vacancy between tenants, and management fees if you're not planning to handle tenant calls yourself. At Orange County price points, even small percentage costs add up to real dollars fast.

Selling and renting are both legitimate paths. The mistake is picking one without actually running your numbers.
